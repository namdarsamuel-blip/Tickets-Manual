01 · Tools of the Trade

Software & Platforms

Where tickets get bought (primary), where they get sold (secondary), and the tools that watch the primary sites for you. We buy manually — no bots — so speed and the right tooling are what let us compete.

TM
Ticketmaster
Primary marketplace
  • Main primary seller for most major tours and arenas
  • Also runs "Verified Resale" — the Standard Reseller program for listing tickets back on TM itself
More info
  • Face Value Exchange is a fan-return listing, not a broker resale program. A fan who can't make it to the show posts their seat back onto Ticketmaster itself, at exactly the price they originally paid — non-transferable, no markup, and no countdown timer on the listing. That's also why Phantom and Ticketmaster label it as its own "ticket type."
  • No timer means no time to deliberate once you decide to act — but a Face Value Exchange hit on the checker is not automatically a buy. People have bought these assuming it must be good, then checked the market and found it was actually a pass. For a market you already know well (e.g. Noah Kahan), you can often tell it's a buy instantly; for shows you don't know as well, it still hits the checker as Face Value Exchange whether or not it's actually worth buying — quickly sanity-check the market before committing.
  • Delivery type must be set to Mobile, never Will Call. Will Call requires the buyer to physically pick up/check in, so there's no transferable code and the ticket can't be resold.
  • Most checkouts give a 7–8 minute timer, but some shows have no timer at all — be ready to decide immediately on those.
  • Doesn't always flag obstructed seats on its own. Always disclose if a seat is side view, limited view, partially obstructed, or video view (see Glossary for each) — an undisclosed obstruction risks a chargeback where the buyer keeps the seat and gets refunded. The principle: buy like you're the customer, not the broker — if you wouldn't be happy sitting there without knowing in advance, disclose it.
  • VIP packages and "official platinum" listings (marked with an orange star icon) are rarely worth buying — usually skipped 90%+ of the time — but it depends on the specific show, not a blanket rule. Don't rule them out automatically: if the official platinum price is close to standard pricing (e.g. only ~$20 more), it can be worth buying. Check the gap before skipping past them.
  • Video view seats (no view of the stage at all, just a screen) are essentially never bought, but worth noting: venues generally only release video view seats for top-tier artists in the first place. So if you see video view seats on a map, treat that as a signal the show is extremely in-demand — people are willing to pay just to be in the building and watch a screen.
  • Wheelchair-accessible seats are rarely bought — only for an exceptionally hot show.

  • Used as a primary ticket source for some venues/events

  • Currently very difficult to use — proxy issues are making it essentially non-functional to open links on. In practice, most buying right now is Ticketmaster and SeatGeek only.

  • Used occasionally for concerts held at baseball stadiums (not for baseball games themselves)
  • Inventory here is rare and the platform can be inconsistent — don't expect this to be a regular source
  • Has its own checkout errors to watch for, on top of being a rare source — don't assume a smooth checkout

Pacolan
Primary marketplace (unreliable)
  • Another primary platform we occasionally buy on
  • Notoriously unreliable at checkout — errors are common, and some shows simply can't be checked out on this platform at all despite repeated attempts
  • Still worth trying when it's the source for a show, but go in expecting friction rather than a clean process
SH
StubHub
Secondary marketplace
  • Use for Canadian shows and Broadway shows specifically
  • Not VividSeats for these — StubHub is the preferred secondary
VS
VividSeats
Secondary marketplace
  • The secondary market we check to compare pricing and get a sense of where a ticket will land
  • For Canadian/Broadway shows, use StubHub instead
More info
  • Prices show with fees baked in by default — always toggle fees off before comparing, or the number you see will be inflated.
  • Example: Ticketmaster shows $100, VividSeats shows $170 with fees on — but with fees off it's actually $140. You'll end up selling around $140, not $170, so pricing off the fee-inclusive number is a common, costly mistake.
  • Default to checking quantity 2 when comparing comps — a single ticket doesn't show the full picture, and singles can be priced misleadingly cheaper than pairs. That said, pair is just the default lens for comparison — we actually buy a range of quantities, not only pairs, so it's worth checking other quantities too, not just defaulting to two and stopping there. Use quantity 4 if there are a lot of seats available for a fuller view of the floor price.
  • Watch the currency — for a Canadian show, convert your Ticketmaster cost from CAD to USD first, then compare to VividSeats (which is usually already in USD). Team leads generally want cost-per-ticket reported in USD, so converting your own cost rather than the comp side keeps that consistent.
PH
Phantom
Drop checker / monitor tool
  • Monitors Ticketmaster and other sites for new ticket drops, price drops, and Face Value Exchange releases — sends direct links so you can act immediately
  • Announces new seat releases, Face Value Exchange listings, and major price drops as they happen
More info
  • Stock checker (purple indicator): admins configure alerts for specific sections — when available inventory drops below a set threshold, it highlights in purple as "low stock." Used for large-supply sections like lawns where you'd wait for 4,000+ tickets to sell through before committing to the remaining inventory.
  • We buy manually, not with bots, so Phantom is what lets us move fast enough to compete when something drops.
  • Running multiple checkers: common to keep two checker windows open at once — e.g. one unfiltered, one filtered to "new seat drops only" with a minimum ticket count (e.g. 20+) so it only surfaces bigger releases. This is personal preference; the tradeoff is that filtering out small drops means you might miss a good pair, but reduces noise so you don't miss a big release while distracted.
  • Custom alert sounds: Phantom lets you assign a distinct sound to specific shows/artists you're watching closely, so you immediately recognize a hit without needing to look at the screen.
  • Searching a specific show: use the URL/ID for a show (found in its Ticketmaster link) in Phantom's search bar to filter the checker down to just that event across all its cities/dates, rather than scrolling the full unfiltered feed.
  • Compressed vs. expanded view: expanded view shows more detail per drop (useful when you want to evaluate before acting); compressed view favors speed once you already know what you're looking for.
JC
JANCY
The browser we buy on
  • Holds all your accounts/profiles — each one has a name, email, and ticket limit
  • Used for both presale links (from the Google Sheet) and for buying on drops you spot in the checker
  • Drag or paste the listing URL into JANCY's URL box, select which accounts to launch, and it opens that page across all of them at once
More info
  • Ticket limit is the max tickets a single account can buy on one order — usually around 8, sometimes 4–6 or up to 50. Check it on the Ticketmaster/FIFA map itself (right-side panel shows the max quantity selector) rather than guessing.
  • Launching means opening a listing across multiple accounts at once. For a big drop (e.g. 500 seats), you launch across many accounts to actually move real volume — usually 10–12 accounts is workable, since launching too many at once (past ~24) slows everything down. But even a small drop (a pair, or a 10-seat drop) is worth launching on more than one account as a hedge — some accounts just won't see certain inventory (see "Some accounts don't see it" below), so launching wider increases the odds at least one account gets through, even when you don't need the extra quantity.
  • Once launched, work through the accounts one at a time: add to cart, move to next, don't stop to check whether you got it — speed matters more than confirming each one in the moment. An account turning yellow means it's loading/carding; green means confirmed in cart; if it stays blue, you didn't get it.
  • When a batch of accounts is done (all carded or failed), double-click to clear the selected accounts and relaunch the same link to keep going until inventory runs out.
  • Two-window setup: Some people run two JANCY windows simultaneously — one dedicated to dragging in links, one for launching accounts. Keeps the workflow cleaner when drops are coming fast.
  • Ghost hits: Sometimes a seat you already bought will re-appear on the checker as if it dropped again. This is a known Ticketmaster/Face Value Exchange glitch — it's not a real new drop, just the same seat appearing again. You'll start to recognize the pattern over time.
  • JANCY's auto-clicker auto-selects seats at the same price point and adds them to cart automatically. If it stops responding correctly, use Ctrl+/ to toggle "seat automation" off, click manually, then Ctrl+/ again to re-enable it.
  • Ticket limit is enforced by the underlying site (e.g. Ticketmaster), not by JANCY — trying to exceed it gives the same "you requested too many tickets" error whether you're buying through JANCY or directly on the site.
AH
Account hygiene
Keeping accounts usable long-term
  • Close tabs you're not actively using. Leaving many tabs open and inactive is a known cause of accounts getting blocked — Ticketmaster flags that kind of inactivity pattern.
  • When an account gets blocked, you can't fix it immediately — tag it with the reason and set it aside. Some come back after a cooldown (minutes to a day); others stay blocked, in some cases permanently.
More info
  • Accounts are listed alphabetically by first name in JANCY and can't be manually reordered — over time, accounts further down the list (later in the alphabet) tend to get used less, which is actually useful: rotate to less-used accounts when your usual ones are getting heavily used, so you always have some "fresher" accounts in reserve rather than running all of them equally hard.
  • If an account gets blocked, it's not necessarily gone forever — letting it sit unused for a while can sometimes restore it. Tagging blocked accounts with the reason helps track which ones are cooling down vs. permanently dead.
  • Fastcart accounts are a subset of accounts that skip the insurance prompt at checkout entirely, saving a step — useful for true no-timer situations where every second counts. They're harder to get/maintain than regular accounts (not necessarily more expensive, just trickier to keep working) and can cause problems if overused, so we deliberately keep only some accounts as fastcart rather than converting all of them.
TC
Team communication
Reporting workflows via team chat
  • If you bought a limited view, side view, video view, or wheelchair seat, report it in team chat with a screenshot — this isn't optional, it's how the disclosure step on the listing actually gets handled.
  • For a big or uncertain drop, it's normal to screenshot your cart and ask a team lead for a thumbs up/down before completing the purchase, rather than guessing.
More info
  • If you used a personal Citi (or other) card for a City-card-locked presale, report that purchase so it gets reimbursed — don't assume it's tracked automatically.
  • If you check something and it's clearly not worth buying (e.g. VividSeats pricing is a pass), flag it so it can be filtered out of the checker — this keeps the checker from repeatedly surfacing inventory that nobody's going to act on.
  • New artist tour announcements get posted to team chat as they come in — worth keeping an eye on for early awareness of upcoming presales/drops.

Keyboard shortcuts
For moving fast in JANCY/Ticketmaster
  • Ctrl + Tab (click repeatedly, don't hold) — move forward through open tabs
  • Ctrl + Shift + Tab — move backward through tabs
  • Ctrl + / — toggle JANCY's seat automation on/off
More info
  • F5 — refresh the page. Useful if a section the checker flagged isn't showing up yet (sometimes you load the link before Ticketmaster has actually posted the seats). Refresh sparingly — repeated refreshing on one account can get that account temporarily or permanently blocked from buying, since it can look like bot/broker behavior rather than a normal fan browsing.
  • Ctrl + 2 — autofill. On a checkout field (CVV, name, address, etc.), autofills using that account's saved info. Used on a blank area of the page (not inside a specific field) it autofills the entire form at once. Doesn't always populate every field on every site (FIFA in particular sometimes needs a few fields filled manually).
  • Ctrl + F — search the page. Useful for finding a specific section/row the checker flagged (e.g. search "406") when it's not obviously visible on the seat map or in the price list.
  • Ctrl + T — open a new tab. Ctrl + W — close the current tab. Basic, but worth having muscle memory for since you're constantly opening/closing tabs while working a drop.
  • Right-click a tab → close tabs to the right — quick way to clear a batch of open tabs at once.
SR
Finding a flagged section
When the checker hits but you don't see it
  • Two places to look: the seat map itself, and the side list (split into "lowest price" and "best seats"). The side list is generally more reliable than the map.
  • "Best seats" surfaces the priciest/closest options (can include official platinum or experience packages) — a flagged section won't necessarily show there. Check "lowest price" too.
More info
  • If it's still not showing: refresh once (F5) — sometimes the checker hits before Ticketmaster has actually posted the seats. Don't refresh repeatedly; see the refresh-blocking note under Keyboard shortcuts.
  • Try adjusting the price filter range to narrow in on the price point you're looking for — this can make a section appear on the map that wasn't rendering at the full price range.
  • Some accounts simply never see certain seats — this happens and isn't fully understood; it appears effectively random per account. If one account isn't seeing something after a reasonable effort, move to the next account rather than burning time on one. Usually at least one of several launched accounts will see it.
  • Start with smaller quantities (a pair) while you're still building speed, then work up to bigger packs (four, six) as you get faster — there's no downside to only getting a pair.
CO
Checkout page basics
What to check before placing an order
  • The moment you see the confirmation page ("you got them") — capture it immediately (e.g. Win+Shift+Arrow to snap it into its own window, or screenshot). The page can disappear, so don't wait.
  • Delivery must say Mobile — never Will Call
  • Never select add-ons: insurance, parking, event extras — these are never something we want
  • Double-check the order summary before placing the order — check that it's not accidentally official platinum instead of standard, and check for side view / limited view / video view. Also confirm the cart price matches what Phantom showed — the price can change once something's actually in your cart, so don't assume Phantom's number is the final number. This happens to almost everyone at some point; it's fine, just slow down a notch after.
More info
  • Some listings are locked behind a specific credit card type (e.g. Citi-only). Check which card is required and use the matching account/card rather than guessing.
  • For a true no-timer listing, the trick is: select your quantity, hit the buy button, then immediately use autofill/paste to get through payment as fast as possible — there's no reservation period giving you breathing room like a normal checkout.
  • Use the calculator (built into most keyboards/OS) to get the real cost per ticket — divide the total charged (including tax/fees) by quantity, not the listed per-seat price, since the listed price alone can understate the real cost by a meaningful amount.

FIFA
Primary marketplace (World Cup)
  • Used occasionally, specifically for World Cup tickets
  • Launch from FIFA.com/tournaments or a link in chat, click buy now, complete a captcha to join the queue
More info
  • Has a visible queue that fluctuates — sometimes instant, sometimes 20+ minutes. A longer queue suggests higher demand (more people trying to get in), but isn't a guarantee there's real inventory — it can also mean bots are hitting the site on a pattern (e.g. FIFA tends to drop seats at night).
  • Has a visible countdown timer for how long you can stay on the site before being kicked back to the queue — this has been getting shorter over time (recently as low as ~9 minutes). Ticketmaster and most other platforms don't have this; you can sit on those indefinitely.
  • Once something is in your cart, there's a separate, shorter reservation timer (similar to Ticketmaster's ~8 minutes) before the hold expires.
  • Move deliberately, not frantically — clicking/scrolling too fast is suspected (not confirmed) to trigger a block on the account.
  • Category system: Category 1 is the lower bowl / best seats, Category 2 one level up, Category 3 cheaper seats, Category 4 is the corners/cheapest (e.g. ~$80). A yellow highlight on the map typically indicates Category 1 (close rows), which run well above standard pricing — sometimes several times the price of standard seats. If you see yellow, expect a steep markup.
  • There's a list of cities/markets we don't buy for (mentioned: most matches are US-only, Canada is limited, and several Mexican host cities are off-limits) — check the current list before assuming a match is fair game, since this note wasn't confirmed in detail and the real list should be the source of truth.

02 · Getting In Early

Presales & Drops

The two ways we make money: presales (scheduled windows, usually Tue–Fri) and drops (unscheduled releases we monitor via Phantom any time of day). Presales are where we plan ahead; drops are where we stay sharp in between.

Presales vs. drops
Overview

Presales are scheduled — they happen Tue–Fri at set times, and we know they're coming in advance. We pick the cities and artists we want to target, get the links from the Google Sheet, and launch them in JANCY to join the queue before the window opens.

Drops happen any time. While there are no presales happening, we watch Phantom for new seat releases, Face Value Exchange listings hitting the map, or major price drops — and move immediately if something looks worth buying. About 85–90% of what we buy comes through Ticketmaster; AXS and SeatGeek are occasional.

Weekly presale calendar
Standard pattern

Standard weekly pattern across most shows:

Tuesday — Citi Card member presale

Wednesday — Artist presale (via Live Nation)

Thursday — Venue presale (via Ticketmaster)

Friday — Public on-sale, no code needed

All presales start at 10 AM local time for the show's city — not 10 AM Eastern. What that means for us on Eastern time:

10 AM ET — East Coast shows (Boston, New York, NJ, Philadelphia, Miami)

11 AM ET — Central time shows (Nashville, Texas, Chicago)

12 PM ET — Mountain time shows (Arizona, Utah, Denver)

1 PM ET — Pacific time shows (California, Nevada, Pacific Northwest)

ExceptionThis 10 AM pattern holds roughly 90% of the time, but not always — every so often a presale's whole weekly schedule shifts later across the board (e.g. East Coast at 1 PM, Central at 2 PM, Mountain at 3 PM, Pacific at 4 PM instead of the usual times). Don't assume 10 AM East Coast without checking the specific presale.

Presale launch workflow
Workflow

Links for each presale come from a Google Sheet — not from Phantom. You copy the link from the sheet, launch it in JANCY (the browser we use for presales), and it puts you in the queue. Once the window opens, you'll get a queue position that counts down toward your turn — something like "you're 10,000 away," then "1,000 away," then "100 away." This gives you a real-time read on whether you're likely to get in. A juiced account means you're near the top of that queue from the start.

Account-specific or code-specific presales
Type

Some presales require you to buy using the specific account that signed up (account-specific), or require a specific code tied to that signup (code-specific). Either way, track exactly which account or code was used to sign up so you buy with the right one later.

Generic codes
Type

Same code works for everyone — not tied to a specific account. You can get it from someone else (e.g. via ShowsOnSale.com) without having signed up yourself. Usually not reliable for high-demand inventory since everyone has the same code.

Juiced account
Watch for

An account that gets into a presale queue unusually early — a noticeably lower number than other accounts when the presale opens. If you find one of these, tag it "juiced" and set it aside immediately. Don't use it for drops, and don't use it for other presales either — save it specifically for a top-tier presale where you need to move fast and get the best seats. Using a juiced account casually wastes its value.

Artist presale
Type

The presale you sign up for directly. Runs through Live Nation (which owns Ticketmaster). Usually the Wednesday window in the weekly pattern.

Venue presale
Type

Has its own unique code. Harder to find but tends to be generic once sourced — the bottleneck is getting the code, not competing for inventory. Runs through Ticketmaster. Usually the Thursday window.

Citi Card member presale
Type

Requires a Citi card to access. The card on hand sometimes doesn't have enough funds when the window opens — worth maintaining your own Citi card rather than relying on someone else's. Usually the Tuesday window.

ShowsOnSale.com
Resource

The site we find generic presale codes on — widely considered one of the most valuable tools in this business once you learn to read it well. Has a companion Excel export with everything on the site in spreadsheet form. There's also a shared presale sheet the office uses, where the team lead posts links to specific presales as they come up (e.g. "10 AM presale, here's the link"). Reading ShowsOnSale well is a skill your team lead will walk you through in more depth once you're further along — it's not something to expect to master on day one.

AI can find presale codes
Tip

Worth knowing: AI tools are generally quite good at finding presale codes if you ask. Not guaranteed to work every time, but a reasonable thing to try if you're stuck looking for one.

03 · Evaluating a Buy

Buying Strategy

This is the part that's still being figured out as we go — how to tell if a ticket is actually worth buying. These are working notes, not fixed rules.

Auto-buy thresholds — example from Noah Kahan
Example

The price at which something becomes a clear auto-buy vs. "check VividSeats first" depends entirely on the specific artist and how well you know their market. As an example, for Noah Kahan specifically:

Under ~$200 (lowers): Auto-buy, no need to check.

~$200–$350 (lowers): Probably still a buy, quick gut-check if something feels off.

~$400+ or upper levels at higher prices: Check VividSeats first.

The pointThese specific numbers only apply to a show where you already know the market. For a different artist or a market you're less familiar with, the thresholds will be completely different. The skill is building that familiarity over time so you can make the call quickly when something drops.

Ticket types to recognize on the checker
Reference

Face Value Exchange: Fan posting their own seat back at cost, no timer — usually auto-buy territory if the price is right (check if you're uncertain).

Verified Resale: A resale listing running through Ticketmaster's own resale system — also generally a safer auto-buy candidate at reasonable prices.

Standard: Original Ticketmaster inventory still from the primary release. Worth a check on VividSeats before buying.

Make a Difference: Charity-linked tickets — more expensive than regular seats because a portion goes to a charity. Not a standard buy. Check before touching these.

Official Platinum / VIP packages: Priced well above standard. Rarely worth buying unless pricing gap to standard is unusually small.

Day-of-show market volatility
Watch out

Markets can move sharply on the day of a show — prices might crash as sellers panic, or spike as buyers scramble last minute. Even for a show/market you know well and would normally auto-buy, it's worth checking VividSeats on the actual show day before committing, since the number in your head from yesterday may no longer be accurate.

Core method: compare the spread
Strategy

Start with the exact section/row that dropped on Ticketmaster and find the matching ("mirroring") section on VividSeats, fees off. If the prices are close, it's probably not worth buying — there's no room for profit. If VividSeats is running meaningfully higher, that gap may represent real margin.

Don't stop at the mirroring section — check every comparable and better section too (the same tier elsewhere in the venue, the level above, the floors, the primos). Think like a fan choosing between options: if a much better section is the same price as yours on VividSeats, no fan would buy yours over that one, so your seat may not actually move even if the raw spread looks fine. The seat is only a good buy if it holds up against everything a fan could choose instead.

Don't buy into an empty map
Watch out

Even if VividSeats pricing looks great across every comparable section, check the actual Ticketmaster map before buying. If the whole map is still sitting untouched, that's a reason for caution — it could mean the show hasn't picked up yet (possible if tickets just went on sale and you're early), or it could mean fans simply aren't buying at that price anywhere. A cleared-out map alongside good VividSeats pricing is a much stronger signal than good pricing alone.

Sales data: TicketMetric & DataIQ
Strategy

Once VividSeats pricing and the map both look reasonable, sales data is the tiebreaker. Search the artist and specific date/city, then look at: sold in the last day (or last 30 days for a show months out), total quantity sold since on-sale, and ATP (average ticket price).

DataIQ (broker sales via Automatic — most brokers use Automatic, so this is the most accurate for mainstream shows) vs. TicketMetric (fan + broker sales via SeatGeek and StubHub — better for niche shows with thin broker activity, and shows individual recent sales by section/row). Check the other if the first looks borderline.

DataIQ charts view: shows ATP trend over time, minimum sale price, and total tickets sold — useful for seeing if the market is holding steady, climbing, or softening. The minimum sale line getting lower over time is a caution sign; a stable or rising ATP alongside growing total sales volume is a strong market signal.

TicketMetric artist-data feature: shows where an artist has the most monthly listeners/followers by city — useful before a presale to identify which cities to prioritize. If an artist has a Vancouver and a Chicago show, seeing Chicago is a far stronger market helps focus effort.

StubHub as a second opinion
Strategy

When VividSeats pricing seems unusually high or just off, StubHub is worth a quick cross-reference — especially for Canadian shows where StubHub is already the primary secondary marketplace. It's not a replacement for VividSeats as the default comp tool, but a useful sanity check when something looks suspect.

Compare against the artist's other shows and market
Strategy

Sort an artist's full list of upcoming shows by total quantity sold to see how a specific date/city ranks against their other tour stops — a show in the top few by sales is a strong market for that artist; a show near the bottom is weaker, even if the spread looks fine in isolation. City/market quality itself is also a factor worth learning over time — some markets reliably perform well for most artists, others rarely do, independent of the specific show.

Mainly forThis is used more for deciding which presales/cities to focus on than for evaluating individual drops, though it can occasionally be useful for a drop decision too.

Reading sales velocity
Strategy

Which sales number matters depends on how far out the show is. For a show months away, look at 30-day and total-since-onsale — that tells you whether the show has built sustained momentum, which matters for how comfortable you'd be picking up a larger quantity if a good drop came along. For a show happening today or tonight, the 30-day number is irrelevant — what matters is sales in the last 24 hours, since that's your read on whether there's enough live heat to flip a ticket before the show starts.

Buying for a same-day show
Strategy

When a show is hours away, you're weighing the price spread against how much the show is selling right now. There's no fixed number that means "enough" — it depends on the show, the venue, and how big the drop itself is — but the comparison is relative: a section selling dozens of tickets in the last day suggests real ability to move a ticket quickly, while something like single digits in the last day suggests there isn't enough live demand to count on a fast flip, even if the spread looks attractive. A small, controlled drop (a handful of seats) is also a better sign than a large one, since a big release can flood that section and crash the price — what looks like a real spread right now could be gone by tomorrow once more inventory hits the market.

Venue fit vs. artist popularity
Strategy

One input among several: check the artist's popularity (e.g. Spotify monthly listeners) against the size of the venue. An artist with a large following playing a comparatively small venue suggests low supply/high demand. An artist who may struggle to fill a much larger venue (stadium vs. their actual draw) is a sign the show might not sell out, which adds risk. Venue types worth knowing: stadiums, arenas, outdoor amphitheaters, GA (general admission), and theaters. This is one factor among others, not a standalone answer.

Buy like the customer, not the broker
Strategy

When comparing seats within a price break (the same section split into different price tiers depending on row), evaluate it the way an actual fan would: would you personally pay the extra cost to sit closer, or would you rather save money further back? If two tiers are close in price, take the better one — the closer rows usually hold more resale value. If the gap is small and the cheaper option is a meaningfully better deal, the cheaper tier can be the smarter buy. There's no fixed formula — it's a judgment call on what a fan would actually choose.

Not always either/orA common instruction is "go for the price break" — meaning specifically target the point in a section where the price drops to a cheaper tier. But sometimes both tiers are worth buying: the closer rows can resell well on their own even if you also take the cheaper tier behind them.

Know the market before it drops
Strategy

For a tour you already know well, you don't need to check comps in the moment — you already know roughly where that market sits. This matters most for Face Value Exchange drops: when the map for a show is mostly cleared out and you already know, say, the cheapest ticket in the building is running around $230, you can recognize a Face Value Exchange seat as worth grabbing and buy it instantly, since there's no timer and no time to check VividSeats once it appears.

Keep it freshRe-check markets you think you know regularly — ideally daily, maybe a couple times a day for active ones. A market can crash overnight (see Market flood), and if you're working off a price in your head from yesterday, you can badly misjudge a drop today.

Shows we generally skip
Note

Coachella is a notable example we don't buy — both because it runs extremely expensive and because it isn't sold on Ticketmaster, which is where most of what we buy comes from. Worth keeping in mind that a show being huge/in-demand doesn't automatically make it one we'd buy into; platform availability and our usual cost structure matter too.

Friday/Saturday shows tend to perform best
Pattern

All else equal, shows happening on a Friday or Saturday night tend to be the strongest sellers — that's generally the preferred night for a show to fall on, worth factoring in when judging how confident to be in a given date.

Know your real cost per ticket
Watch out

The listed per-seat price isn't the real cost — taxes and fees add to the total charged. Divide the actual total charged by the quantity to get the true cost per ticket before deciding whether something's a buy. The difference is usually small enough that it won't flip a clear buy into a pass, but it matters for accurately reporting cost and margin.

Hot-show exception: singles, wheelchairs, obstructed views
Example

The general guidance is to avoid singles, wheelchairs, and obstructed/side-view seats for seated shows. For an extremely in-demand artist, that guidance can flip — these seat types can sell briskly even close to showtime. Treat this as an exception for genuinely hot shows, not a reason to default into buying these types broadly.

Flex up / flex down
Pattern

If you know a show's pricing well and notice a section's price has dropped from what you remember (e.g. the last several rows of a $400 section are now $160), that's a flex down — worth flagging to the team since it can mean either the show isn't selling well at that price (a caution sign) or the show is confident enough in demand elsewhere that they're willing to discount this section to move it (which can still be a good buy). A flex up is the same thing in reverse — a price increase on a section. Neither is automatically good or bad; the read depends on the specific show and what else is happening with sales.

"Cheaps" — the lowest price tickets ever released
Term

"Cheaps" refers to the lowest-priced tickets a show released at the very start, not whatever the lowest price on the map happens to be right now. If the cheaps already sold out, what looks like the "cheapest available" today might actually be a higher tier — the true cheaps are gone. Checking sales data for what the show dropped at originally is how you find out what the cheaps actually were.

"Sub-10" / "sub-100" — usually means rows, not dollars
Term

When a team lead says "go for sub-10" or "sub-100," they're almost always talking about row numbers, not price — sub-10 means rows under 10 (i.e. close rows). If they actually mean a dollar amount, they'll usually say "sub $200" explicitly with the dollar sign spoken. Don't default to assuming a bare number means price.

Non-transferable tickets
Watch for

Some artists require their tickets to be non-transferable — meaning officially, only the original buyer can attend, and the ticket can't be resold or handed off. However, several states have banned that restriction entirely, meaning even on a "non-transferable" tour, tickets can legally be transferred for shows in those states: New York, Connecticut, Colorado, Illinois, Virginia, and Utah. So a show being labeled non-transferable doesn't automatically rule it out if it's happening in one of these states. We still buy non-transferable inventory more broadly too, using other fulfillment methods — but knowing the state-exception list is useful context regardless.

Early picks
Advanced

An "early pick" is a way of getting notified by the drop checker the moment a ticket someone else is buying becomes available again — before it's actually visible on the Ticketmaster map. If a seat is sitting in someone else's cart (e.g. during a no-timer purchase) and their checkout fails or times out, the seat gets released back to inventory; an early pick alert lets you act on that release immediately, ahead of when it would normally re-appear to everyone else. This is an advanced technique — not something to focus on early in training, just useful to recognize the term.

Market familiarity is the core skill
Strategy

The hardest thing to do is buy into a show for an artist you've never heard of. You don't know what their market looks like, what sections move, what price points make sense, or how to prioritize when a large drop hits with seven different price points. The number one thing you can do early on is play catchup on market knowledge — look at what shows are coming up, open the maps, check secondary, follow along with what's dropping and how the team calls it, even if you're not buying. Every time you watch a drop happen and see someone make a call, you're building the pattern recognition that makes the next decision faster.

Why it mattersWhen a drop hits with 1,000 seats across seven price points, if you've never seen the show you're carding blind on gut instinct. If you've been watching the show, you already know: I like these $130 lowers, I'm not interested in the $187 lowers, let me go for those first.

How to build market awareness proactively
Strategy

The drop checker organizes everything by what's active — look at what's on there within the next two weeks. Those are the shows most likely to have seat releases coming. Open the maps, check VividSeats, look at DataIQ/TicketMetric. You don't have to buy to learn — just be aware of what's happening. Questions to ask yourself for each upcoming show: How are the Charlotte shows doing? What's the Nashville map look like? What's moved, what hasn't? Which sections are sitting dead? If a release happens, would you know what to card? The team moves fast — if you've done your homework, you already have a plan. If you haven't, you're scrambling.

Week-of releases by venue type
Reference

Stadiums: 100% guaranteed to have a release the week of the show. Every stadium show, without exception, will release additional seats within the week before. This is the one category you can count on. Plan around it — if a stadium show is coming up, there will be a drop, and that's the moment to know the market cold.

Amphitheaters: Typically yes, but not guaranteed. They usually release something in the days before, but it doesn't always happen.

Arenas: Sometimes yes, sometimes no. Can't bank on it.

Theaters: Least likely to have a week-of release. Don't count on it.

ExampleAny show in the Chris Brown / Usher tour — every single one of them has dropped at least 1,000 seats within the week of the show. That's tour-level pattern recognition that helps you know a drop is coming before it happens.

New sections the drop checker can't catch
Watch out

Phantom and all drop checkers can only monitor what already exists on the map. If a venue section hasn't been added to Ticketmaster yet — like a floor section that hasn't been released — the checker has nothing to watch. It's not looking for it because it doesn't exist. When those sections eventually drop, they often won't trigger any drop checker alert at all, and a lot of people miss them entirely.

The workaround: Set up a manual filter on the show with a max price — e.g. if you expect floors to drop around $200–300, set a max price of $200. When and if something drops below that threshold, it will hit your filter even though it didn't exist before. If it drops for $201, it won't hit — the filter only catches what falls inside the range. This requires knowing ahead of time approximately what price point a section might drop at.

ExampleChris Brown / Usher in Missouri — floor sections hadn't been added to the map yet. Anyone following the tour would know floors are guaranteed to drop eventually, but no drop checker would catch the moment they first appeared unless a price filter was set up in advance.

Reading a presale for a new / unknown tour
Strategy

When an established artist does a new tour with existing shows to reference (e.g. the Eagles at the Sphere when multiple prior shows already happened), you can base your presale buy decisions on what those earlier shows look like — what prices, what sections, what sales velocity. That's the easy case.

When it's a brand new tour with no reference data — like if Justin Bieber announced a new tour tomorrow — it's much harder. You have to rely on: (1) Spec sales and ATP from DataIQ/TicketMetric to gauge demand as soon as they start populating. High ATP + strong spec sales = can be more aggressive on certain cities. Low ATP + okay specs = more cautious. (2) Comparison against similar artists at similar venues. (3) City-by-city variation — some cities on a tour will have strong spec sales but low ATP (strong demand for cheap seats), others will have both. Each city warrants a separate read. The presale call for City A and City B on the same tour can be completely different.

Playing safe vs. playing aggressive
Reference

Early on — especially when buying drops rather than presales, and without a large cushion of existing profitable inventory to fall back on — playing conservatively is not only okay, it's correct. If you go aggressive and it crashes, there's nothing to absorb the loss. The downside is real and immediate.

As your inventory and track record build, you can start making larger, more aggressive plays because: (1) you have cushion to absorb a loss if it doesn't work, (2) you've developed a gut for which gambles are worth taking, (3) when an aggressive play works, the upside can be massive. One right aggressive call can cover several losses.

What aggressive looks like: Taking on a large quantity of an uncertain show because you see something in the data or the pattern that suggests high upside — knowing your downside if it fails is real but manageable. Example: going heavy into a presale for a new tour you believe in based on spec sales and ATP, even though you don't have reference show data.

Reality checkExperienced buyers in this office have 5K–12K in losses (eating inventory) per month on average. That's part of the game. The goal is that your wins are bigger than your losses — not that you have no losses.

Eating inventory
Term

"Eating" tickets means the inventory sold for nothing — zero recovery. The show happened, you couldn't sell the tickets in time, and you have no recourse. Total loss, not even a partial recovery. This happens to everyone at every level. The goal isn't to never eat seeds — it's to have a win rate and margin that makes the occasional eat manageable rather than devastating. Example: buying aggressively into a presale that crashes. If you have enough cushion, eating 2K worth of inventory is painful but survivable. If you don't have cushion, it's potentially crippling.

December is the best month
Strategy

December is consistently the highest-volume month in this business — not just because it's the holiday season, but specifically because people buy tickets as gifts for shows happening months later. Someone who wanted to see Morgan Wallen in August gets two Morgan Wallen tickets for Christmas at $700 each. This means December sees unusually high sales for shows that are still far out — inventory that has been sitting starts moving, and things sell that wouldn't have moved in October or November. Whatever inventory hasn't sold by late December typically starts to crash afterward as the gift-buying window closes. Plan your inventory accordingly — December is when things clear.

VividSeats listings ≠ actual sales
Watch out

Everything you see listed on VividSeats is what sellers are asking for — not what buyers are actually paying. A section with tickets listed at $250 does not mean anyone is buying at $250. It means someone is trying to sell at $250. The actual sales data — what people paid — is in DataIQ and TicketMetric. This is why you always cross-reference listing prices with actual ATP and sales velocity before concluding a market is good. A section listed high on secondary is only meaningful if sales data confirms people are actually transacting at those levels.

Rescheduled shows — full framework
Strategy

When a show gets rescheduled, it will never be identical to what it was. It will either become significantly better or significantly worse. There is no neutral outcome. Key factors to evaluate:

1. How much time is being given? A reschedule to the next night is almost always the worst case — people already made plans for the original night, many will just take the refund rather than rearrange. A reschedule to two months later gives the market time to rebuild. A reschedule to a year later (like Cody Johnson in Columbus) can turn a struggling show into a double. The more time, the better the potential upside.

2. Does the day of week improve or worsen? A Wednesday show that gets pushed to Saturday is a meaningful upgrade — better attendance night = better market. A Thursday or Friday show pushed to the following Wednesday is a downgrade.

3. How was the tour/show performing before? If the show was already struggling before the reschedule (low sales velocity, prices falling, poor ATP), the reschedule doesn't fix that. If the show was already strong, a reschedule can create a buying opportunity as original buyers cancel and inventory returns to the market at face value or below.

4. Why did it get rescheduled? Weather/logistics = temporary and recoverable. Artist illness = could be a brief pause or could be tour-ending. If it's unclear, wait for more information before committing. Artist health issues that later led to tour cancellation (Billy Joel, Aerosmith) show why you need to read the situation carefully rather than automatically treating a reschedule as a buying opportunity.

5. Venue market dynamics. Vegas is built on tourism — people plan Vegas trips months in advance, and when a show moves from this week to next week, those trip plans don't move with it. A reschedule in Vegas from Thursday to the following Wednesday is extremely unfavorable: the people who built their trip around Thursday's show aren't coming back next Wednesday, and new people haven't had time to plan a Vegas trip around it.

6. When tickets come back to market. When a show reschedules, Ticketmaster typically gives fans a 24–48 hour window to request a refund. Many fans cancel — especially if the original date worked for their schedule but the new one doesn't. Those returned tickets re-enter the market. If a show is rescheduled to a favorable date with good demand, those returned tickets at face value or below are buying opportunities. If the show is in trouble, all those returned tickets will further flood an already weak secondary market.

Worked examplesNoah Kahan amphitheater reschedule (amphitheater tour, favorable conditions): 400 lawns purchased in ~24 hours as fans canceled and no cart hold was in place — one of the best lawn buying opportunities ever seen. Kehlani at Camden (rescheduled, eventually may have canceled): cheaper upper seats ($47–57 center uppers) worked; $150 pits did not — market specific. Cody Johnson Columbus (rescheduled a full year later): was struggling with $150 floors selling for $30, but after a year's delay those shows doubled. Aerosmith (reschedule due to lead singer Tyler's throat cancer): any original-price floor drop was an automatic buy — shows had flexed up from presale price, and the dropped seats were the original pre-flex-up price coming back, which the whole team knew to buy without hesitation. Billy Joel collapse (rescheduled then tour canceled): pull back, not a buying opportunity. Corbin Carroll / Korean Leon at Sphere (rescheduled from Thursday to next Wednesday in Vegas): expected to go poorly — tour wasn't performing well, and Vegas tourism dynamics work against a near-same-week reschedule.

Residency
Term

A residency is when an artist performs multiple shows at the same venue, often repeatedly over an extended period rather than moving city to city on a tour. Most commonly associated with Vegas — artists like Backstreet Boys and Adele have had Vegas residencies, meaning they perform at the same venue (e.g. the Sphere or Caesars) for many consecutive shows rather than doing a traditional touring circuit. Billy Joel had a monthly residency at MSG (Madison Square Garden) for years. The last shows of a residency sometimes have extra demand (closing weekend effect) which can inflate sales numbers in ways that aren't representative of the overall run.

04 · Reading a Seating Chart

Venue Section Map

The naming conventions used for sections in an arena, and the disclosure types that affect what you can sell a seat as. This becomes second nature fast — it's mostly about building a shared vocabulary with the team.

Arena section names (lower bowl)
Reference

Primos — the sideline sections, generally the best/highest-value seats in the lower bowl.

Corners — exactly what it sounds like, the corner sections moving out from primos. Generally hold less resale value than primos.

Back bowl — the back of the lower bowl. Some consider this better than corners since you're facing the stage straight-on rather than at an angle, even though it's further back.

NoteExactly which sections count as primo vs. corner vs. back bowl varies by venue — there's no universal section-number rule. Get a feel for each specific venue's layout rather than assuming the boundaries transfer between arenas.

Reading the seat map
Reference

Two ways to grab seats: directly off the visual map (zoom in, seats appear as colored dots you click), or off the side list view (a text list of available seats next to the map). The side list is generally more reliable, since the visual map can occasionally glitch or lag. Some people deliberately refresh/jiggle the map view as a trick to get stuck listings to pop back up.

Dot colors on the map (Ticketmaster, unfiltered by JANCY pricing):

Blue — standard seating at standard price. This is what you're buying most of the time.

Pink with an arrow/symbol — a resale listing, meaning someone (possibly another broker, possibly a fan) already bought this seat and is reselling it through Ticketmaster's own resale system. We generally avoid these — the markup is usually steep relative to the standard seat right next to it.

Star symbol — a VIP/package listing, often bundled with extras (meet-and-greet, etc.). Priced well above standard — often more than double. We rarely buy these; if a VIP package were actually worth grabbing, fans would typically have already taken it before it's sitting available.

Gray — unavailable, either already sold or not released yet. A map with a lot of gray sitting in one area can mean that section hasn't dropped yet and may be released later — worth watching for the pattern.

Floor section names
Reference

Center front floor — center, front, floor (the name describes exactly what it is).

Off-center front floor — same idea, just off to the side rather than dead center.

Center floor / back floor — moving further back from the front floor sections, still on the floor.

Floor sections are almost always standard tickets (not VIP), even though they're close to the stage.

Floor vs. pit"Floor" usually means assigned/seated floor sections, while "pit" means standing room right in front of the stage — but which one a venue actually has at the bottom varies by show; sometimes the entire bottom area is seated floor, sometimes the entire bottom area is a standing pit. Check what's actually being sold rather than assuming.

Uppers
Reference

Everything in the upper level uses the same naming logic as the lower bowl — upper primo/sideline, upper corners, upper back bowl. Larger venues can have multiple upper levels (e.g. 200 level, 300 level) — just refer to them by level number if there's more than one tier.

Disclosure types
Watch for

These all describe a seat that doesn't have a fully normal view of the stage, and all carry the same risk: if you don't disclose it on the listing, an unhappy fan can dispute the charge and you lose the ticket's value twice over (refunded and they keep attending).

Side view / limited / obstructed — used fairly interchangeably for a seat where the view is angled or partially blocked. Usually priced lower than the equivalent straight-on seat. Check the listing's own seat detail (scroll down on the section) to confirm before buying — don't assume.

Partially obstructed — same idea, often shown split within a single section (some seats flagged, others not).

Video view — no view of the stage at all; the seat faces a screen instead. Essentially never bought, except for a top-tier presale on an extremely in-demand artist.

Wheelchair-accessible — rarely bought; only for an exceptionally hot show.

Venue types: arena vs. stadium vs. theater vs. GA
Reference

Arena — mid-size indoor venue. Think of an ice-skating-rink-style building — round/oval bowl shape.

Stadium — massive, think baseball-stadium scale. MSG counts as a stadium-class venue in this sense. An artist doing a "stadium tour" means dramatically more seats per show than an arena tour — useful shorthand when judging how big a run is.

Theater — elongated rather than round, like a Broadway house. Smaller scale, different layout entirely (orchestra, mezzanine, etc.) rather than arena bowl/floor naming.

GA (general admission) — no assigned seats, just a standing-room area. Singles are fine to buy here, unlike seated shows, since a fan can easily pair up with a stranger for GA.

Amphitheaters
Reference

Outdoor venues, used mainly in summer. Layout is simpler than an arena — usually no primos/corners/back bowl naming, just numbered levels: a pit (if there is one), 100 level / center lower, 200 level, 300 level, and lawn at the back. Exact layout varies by venue, but the general idea (closer levels, then lawn) holds across most of them.

Pit — the standing area right in front of the stage, like GA but specifically for the front section. Value depends almost entirely on the specific artist, not the genre — some artists' pits sell for several times the price of standard seats (e.g. pit at $800 when everything else is $100–200), others barely move. EDM/rave-style shows tend to have stronger pit demand (people want to be close to the DJ), as do rock/mosh-pit-culture shows — but this is a loose pattern, not a rule, and it really comes down to the specific artist.

Lawn — open grass seating at the back. Not technically unlimited, but capacity is very large (thousands of seats), and a lawn that looks small on the Ticketmaster map can hold far more people in person than the rest of the venue combined. Because supply is so large, lawns are often not worth buying at full price without a reason to think otherwise. A promo (lawns discounted well below their normal price, e.g. $25 when the standard price is $54) makes a lawn buy more likely — buy at the promo price, sell at or above the normal price, profit from the price gap rather than scarcity. But the absence of a promo doesn't automatically mean pass — for a genuinely hot show, lawns can still be a buy at full price (e.g. an artist whose lawns sold for $500+ on tour). Lawns are a quantity game, not a quality game — a four-pack makes a small profit, but a few hundred lawn tickets adds up fast.

Reseller colors on the map
Reference

On Ticketmaster's seat map, different colors within the same section often represent different sellers/price points rather than different seat quality — e.g. all the orange-highlighted seats might be one reseller's listings at one price, while blue seats are a different reseller at a different price. JANCY's pulled map sometimes shows this color-coding more clearly than the live Ticketmaster page does, which makes it easier to compare price points at a glance.

Row 1 can hold value on its own
Pattern

Front-row seats (Row 1) in a section can sell well even when the rest of that section is sitting completely dead with no movement. Worth checking specifically for Row 1 availability even on a map that otherwise looks unsellable — sometimes that one row is worth taking on its own.

Choosing between equal-priced seats
Pattern

When multiple options are priced the same, prefer the larger pack size — a 4-pack or 6-pack is generally worth more than the equivalent number of seats split into pairs, since a fan looking for a bigger group has fewer listings to choose from. Within a price break, a smaller section with fewer available seats can sometimes be worth taking over a closer-but-larger section, since you're competing against far fewer other sellers for buyer attention.

Back bowl
The back sections of an arena's lower bowl, directly facing the stage.
More info

The lower bowl sections at the back of the arena, furthest from the stage but facing it straight-on rather than at an angle. Generally holds less resale value than primos (sideline sections) but can outperform corners in certain markets. Exact sections that count as "back bowl" vary by venue — there's no universal section-number rule, so get familiar with each arena's specific layout rather than assuming.

Bots
Automated buying tools that competitors use to purchase tickets the instant inventory appears.
More info

Many professional brokers in the industry use bots — software that automatically detects new inventory and completes purchases in milliseconds, far faster than any human can. We buy manually without bots. This means: (1) Phantom and drop checkers are essential since they give us the earliest possible alert, (2) we launch across multiple JANCY accounts simultaneously to compensate for what one human can do vs. multiple bots, (3) there is often no time to deliberate — when something drops for a market you know, the decision has to already be made. Bots are also suspected of being the cause of some Ticketmaster rate-limit blocks when accounts refresh too aggressively.

Broadway
Theater shows — either fixed in NYC or traveling to other cities.
More info

Broadway shows come in two types: fixed (running permanently in NYC, e.g. Hamilton, Phantom of the Opera) and traveling/touring Broadway (the same show visiting different cities across the country, e.g. Lion King appearing in Chicago, LA, etc.). The key operational rule: Broadway shows use StubHub as the secondary marketplace, not VividSeats. This applies to both fixed NYC shows and traveling Broadway. Phantom of the Opera was used as a worked example of this rule early in training.

Canadian show
A concert or event taking place in Canada.
More info

Two important rules for Canadian shows: (1) Use StubHub as the secondary marketplace, not VividSeats. (2) Prices on Ticketmaster for Canadian shows may display in CAD (Canadian dollars), not USD. Always convert your Ticketmaster cost from CAD to USD before comparing to VividSeats (which displays in USD) or reporting your cost-per-ticket to the team — team leads want cost reported in USD. Forgetting to convert is a common mistake that makes a buy look better (or worse) than it actually is.

Cart hold
When a seat is temporarily reserved in your cart — some listings have one, some don't.
More info

When you add a seat to your cart on Ticketmaster, it's usually held (reserved) for you for 7–8 minutes while you complete checkout — this is the cart hold. Some listings, particularly Face Value Exchange, have no cart hold at all. 'No cart hold' means the seat is available to anyone simultaneously and whoever completes checkout first gets it. This is why no-timer + no-cart-hold situations require instant action. Example: Noah Kahan amphitheater reschedule — 400 lawns were purchased in ~24 hours because there was no cart hold, so the team could continuously rip the same listing over and over as returning fans' seats came back immediately.

Chargeback (limited view)
When a buyer disputes a limited-view seat that wasn't disclosed — they get refunded and still attend.
More info

If you sell a seat that has an obstructed sightline (beam, speaker stack, staging, pillar, etc.) and don't disclose it on the listing, the buyer can dispute the purchase. The outcome: they receive a full refund AND still get to attend the show using the ticket. You lose the face value of the ticket plus the seat goes to the buyer for free — a double loss. Ticketmaster doesn't always flag limited-view seats on its own, so you must check the seat detail before buying and always disclose if there's any obstruction. This also applies to side view, partially obstructed, and video view seats — same chargeback risk for any undisclosed obstruction type.

Cheaps
The lowest-priced tickets released at a show's original on-sale — not what's cheapest on the map today.
More info

When a show first goes on sale, it releases tickets at various price tiers. The cheapest of those initial tiers are the "cheaps." Once those sell out, the remaining cheapest available tickets are a higher tier — but people sometimes incorrectly treat today's lowest price as "the cheaps" when the original cheaps are long gone. To find what the real cheaps were, check sales data in DataIQ or TicketMetric for the show's lowest recorded sale prices at launch. This matters when evaluating whether the current secondary market pricing represents a real profit opportunity vs. everyone now competing in higher tiers where margins are tighter.

Requires an active Citi card to access — the presale code or link is only valid for Citi cardholders. The card on hand sometimes doesn't have enough available credit when the window opens (a real-world problem), so team members are encouraged to have their own personal Citi card rather than relying solely on a shared one. Presale link comes from the Google Sheet; launch in JANCY. This is often the first presale window of the week, meaning it's the first chance to buy ahead of the general public.

More info

Coachella is excluded for two reasons: (1) tickets are extremely expensive, making the cost-to-resale margin very difficult to justify, and (2) Coachella doesn't sell through Ticketmaster — it uses its own platform, outside our standard buying workflow. Worth knowing because Coachella comes up as a reference point for "big show we don't touch." The broader lesson: a show being massive and in-demand doesn't automatically make it a buy. Comparing across a tour
Using other shows on the same tour as reference points before buying a specific date/city.
More info

When evaluating whether to buy a specific show, look at how other shows on the same tour are performing — particularly shows that have already happened or are actively selling. If the tour has five shows and four are performing well, you can be more confident about the fifth. If the tour is consistently underperforming city by city (dropping prices, slow sales), that pattern applies to new shows too. Used most in: (1) Presale evaluation — before buying into a presale for a new date, check how the artist's existing tour shows are doing in DataIQ/TicketMetric sorted by total quantity. (2) Rescheduled shows — knowing how the tour performed before is critical input into whether a reschedule is a buying opportunity or a pass. (3) New cities added to a tour — treat like any presale but with reference data already available. Artists like the Eagles at the Sphere gave you 5–6 shows to reference before buying a new date on the same run.

Core buy decision
The full sequence for deciding whether to buy: VividSeats spread → map check → sales data → call.
More info

When something drops, the complete decision process: Step 1 — VividSeats. Toggle fees off, check quantity 2. Find the mirroring section, then check every comparable and better section. Is there real spread? If clearly no, pass immediately. If yes, continue. Step 2 — Map check. Is the whole map still sitting with primary inventory? Fans could buy directly — why would they pay secondary? That weakens the buy. If the map is mostly cleared, secondary is the only option for fans, strengthening it. Step 3 — Sales data. DataIQ or TicketMetric. Are people actually buying? Check sold in last day (same-day show) or last 30 days (months out). Check ATP vs. your cost. Check total volume and tour ranking. Step 4 — Decision. For a market you know cold, steps 1–3 may collapse into a 3-second check or an auto-buy. For an unfamiliar show, work through every step deliberately. All of this happens inside your 7–8 minute checkout timer — often under 60 seconds for experienced buyers.

Corner
The corner sections of an arena's lower bowl — between primos and back bowl in terms of value.
More info

Corners are the angled sections at the corners of the arena bowl, between the sideline (primo) sections and the back-facing sections. The sightline is angled toward the stage rather than straight-on, which is why they generally hold less resale value than primos. In some venues and for some artists, the difference in value between corners and primos is significant; in others it's minimal. Exact section numbers that count as "corner" vary by venue — learn each arena's specific layout. When a team lead says "go for primos, not corners," they mean prioritize the sideline sections over the angled ones.

DataIQ
Sales and pricing tool — shows broker-only sales data, pulled from Automatic.
More info

DataIQ pulls from Automatic, which records broker sales. Because most professional brokers in the industry use Automatic, DataIQ is considered the most accurate tool for mainstream shows with heavy broker activity. Key data points to look at: ATP (average ticket price — what people are actually paying, not listing), sold in last day (most relevant for same-day shows), sold in last 30 days (most relevant for shows months out), and total sold since on-sale. DataIQ also has a charts view showing ATP trend over time, minimum sale price, and total volume — useful for seeing if the market is stable, rising, or declining. Limitation: thin or no data for niche/underground shows where few brokers are active. For those, use TicketMetric instead, which also captures fan sales via SeatGeek and StubHub.

Drop checker
A monitoring tool that alerts you the moment tickets drop, a price changes, or Face Value Exchange seats appear.
More info

We use Phantom as our drop checker. It monitors Ticketmaster and other platforms continuously and fires an alert the moment something notable happens — new seat release, price drop, or Face Value Exchange listing appearing. The alert includes direct links to the listing (e.g. the Ticketmaster link, the VividSeats link for comparison), so you don't waste time searching. Two common setups: (1) one unfiltered checker showing everything, (2) one filtered to new seat drops only with a minimum quantity (e.g. 20+ seats), so big drops are highlighted. Custom sounds can be assigned to specific artists you're watching closely so you recognize a hit without looking at the screen. Phantom's purple "stock checker" alerts (set by admins) fire when a section's remaining inventory drops below a set threshold — useful for lawns and large-supply sections.

Drop vs. presale
Two distinct revenue streams — presales are scheduled in advance, drops happen reactively from the checker.
More info

Presales are scheduled: you know in advance that Tuesday at 10 AM a Citi presale opens, or Wednesday a venue presale drops. You prepare: get the link from the Google Sheet, have accounts ready, research the market beforehand so you know exactly what to buy the moment you're in the queue. Presales give you time to prep. Drops are reactive: Phantom fires an alert, you have seconds to evaluate and act. A drop can happen any time — a venue releases additional seats, Face Value Exchange seats appear, or prices fall below a threshold. Drops require instant pattern recognition built on market familiarity. Early on you're mostly working drops — it's where you build speed and market knowledge. Presales come later as you develop a gut for which shows are worth a larger pre-planned commitment. The best buyers are strong at both: systematic on presales, fast and decisive on drops.

Early pick
An alert that fires the instant a seat someone else was buying gets released back to inventory — before it re-appears publicly.
More info

If another buyer has a seat in their cart and their checkout fails, times out, or they abandon it, the seat gets released back to inventory. An early pick alert notifies you the moment that happens, giving you first access before the seat re-appears on the public map or triggers a standard drop checker alert. This is an advanced technique — not something to focus on early in training. The main point is recognizing the term when you hear it so it's not confusing.

Eating inventory
When tickets don't sell before the show date and become a 100% total loss — no recovery, no partial refund, nothing.
More info

"Eating seeds" or "eating inventory" means the show happened and you couldn't sell the tickets. There is nothing left — no partial recovery, no resale option, no getting any money back. This is the maximum downside outcome. It happens to everyone at every level of experience — even experienced buyers with strong track records eat inventory regularly. The goal is not to avoid it entirely (that's impossible while also playing aggressively enough to make real money) but to keep the losses manageable relative to your wins. At experienced levels, 5K–12K in losses per month is considered normal and expected — the wins should more than compensate. Early on, with less cushion and less track record, the impact of eating inventory is more severe, which is why playing more conservatively is appropriate until you've built up both skill and buffer. Example: buying aggressively into a J. Cole presale, believing it's a strong play, and having ~$2K worth of seats not sell. If you have the cushion, it stings but doesn't break you. If you don't, it's a serious problem.

Face Value Exchange
A fan's own seat posted back onto Ticketmaster at exactly the price they originally paid.
More info

This is Ticketmaster's mechanism for a fan who can't attend to return their seat to the market — not a broker resale tool. The fan goes into their account, selects the option to list the ticket, and it gets reposted at exactly what they paid (face value). It's non-transferable, no markup allowed. The critical distinguishing feature: Face Value Exchange listings have NO countdown timer at checkout — you must complete the purchase immediately without the usual 7–8 minute window. Phantom and Ticketmaster both label these as their own ticket type, so you can spot them quickly. Important: a Face Value Exchange hit on the checker is NOT automatically a buy — it always means no timer, but the market still needs to make sense. For a show you know cold (e.g. Noah Kahan with a cleared-out map at $230+ cheapest seat), you can decide instantly. For an unfamiliar market, do a quick VividSeats check first. Watch for ghost hits — sometimes a seat you already bought re-appears as Face Value Exchange; this is a known glitch, not a real new drop.

Fastcart account
A JANCY account configured to skip the insurance prompt at checkout, saving a step.
More info

On most Ticketmaster checkouts, there's an insurance add-on prompt you have to decline before proceeding. Fastcart accounts bypass this entirely, saving several seconds — which matters most on no-timer checkouts where every second counts. These accounts are harder to maintain than regular accounts and can cause problems if overused or treated carelessly, so only some accounts are kept as fastcart rather than converting all of them. When working a no-timer drop, prioritizing fastcart accounts in your launch order can meaningfully improve your chances of completing the purchase in time.

FIFA
The platform we buy World Cup tickets on — FIFA.com/tournaments.
More info

Process: go to FIFA.com/tournaments (or a link shared in team chat), find the match, click Buy Now, complete a captcha, and you enter the queue. FIFA has a visible queue that fluctuates — sometimes instant, sometimes 20+ minutes. Key differences from Ticketmaster: (1) FIFA has a site-level countdown timer showing how long you can stay before being kicked back to the queue — this has been as short as ~9 minutes recently and keeps getting shorter; most other platforms have no such timer. (2) Once something is in your cart, there's a separate shorter reservation timer (similar to TM's ~8 min) before the hold expires. (3) Category system: Cat 1 = best seats (lower bowl, closest rows) often marked yellow on the map and priced several times higher than standard; Cat 2, Cat 3, Cat 4 = progressively worse seats and cheaper (Cat 4 ~$80). Move deliberately — clicking too fast is suspected (not confirmed) to trigger account blocks. Geographic restrictions apply — there's a list of shows/markets that are off-limits; verify before buying.

Flex up / flex down
A price change on a section after a show's initial pricing — up means increase, down means decrease.
More info

Shows don't always keep the same prices they launched at. A flex down (price decrease on a section) can mean: the show isn't moving tickets at that price point (a caution sign), or the promoter is confident enough in overall demand to discount one section to fill it faster (can still be a fine buy). A flex up (price increase) can mean demand is higher than expected and they're capturing more value. Neither is automatically good or bad — interpret based on everything else you know about the show's sales velocity and the section in question. Always worth flagging to the team when you spot one.

Floor vs. pit
Floor = assigned seated sections at stage level. Pit = standing GA area directly in front of the stage.
More info

These terms are often confused. Floor: the flat area inside the arena bowl at stage level, with assigned seats — sections like Floor A, Floor 1, Center Front Floor. You have a specific seat organized by section/row/seat number. Pit: a standing GA area directly in front of the stage, no assigned spots — first to arrive gets the best position. Whether a show has floor or pit (or both, or neither) varies by artist and event. Some shows convert the entire floor to a standing pit; others keep the floor as assigned seating and add a small pit right at the barricade; others have no pit at all. Always check what's actually being sold rather than assuming. Pit usually commands higher prices than floor for high-demand artists due to proximity to the stage — but value varies enormously by artist. Floor sections: center front floor (highest value), off-center front floor, back floor.

GA (general admission)
A venue or section with no assigned seats — standing room only, first come first served on entry.
More info

No seat numbers, no assigned spots — you buy a ticket to be in the GA area and claim a spot when you arrive. Key buying differences from seated shows: singles are fine to buy for GA (unlike seated shows where singles are hard to sell), since a fan going alone can easily join any spot in a GA crowd. GA sections don't have row/seat numbers so there's no "mirroring section" comp in the same way — you're comparing GA ticket to GA ticket on VividSeats. GA capacity varies widely: a small GA pit at an amphitheater might hold a few hundred people; a GA floor at a festival might hold tens of thousands. GA for a hot show can sell significantly above face value on secondary markets.

Generic code
A presale code that works for anyone — not tied to a specific account or signup.
More info

Unlike account-specific or code-specific presales, a generic code is the same string shared across all buyers for that presale window. You don't need to have signed up yourself — you can get it from someone who has it, from ShowsOnSale.com, or by asking an AI tool. Downside: because the code is widely shared, competition at the moment the presale opens is very high. Usually not the move for in-demand inventory since everyone with the same code rushes in simultaneously. Venue presales often use generic codes — hard to find, but once found, anyone can use them.

Ghost hit
When a seat you already bought re-appears on the drop checker as if it dropped again.
More info

A known Ticketmaster/Face Value Exchange glitch. After you complete a purchase, the system sometimes re-fires the same listing as an alert on Phantom. It's not a real new drop — the seat is already yours. You'll start to recognize the pattern: same section, same price, same show, immediately after you just bought something. If you're unsure, check your purchase confirmation rather than trying to buy again. Ghost hits are more common with Face Value Exchange listings than standard drops.

Not Phantom — presale links come from the Google Sheet. The team lead posts the relevant presale links there (e.g. "Tuesday 10 AM Citi presale for X artist, here's the link"). You copy that link, paste it into JANCY's URL bar, select your accounts, and launch. The Google Sheet is the operational source of truth for which presales are happening and what the links are. Gut and market intuition
Fast, accurate decision-making built from repeated exposure to the same markets — not guessing.
More info

The 'gut' in this business is compressed experience. When an experienced buyer sees a drop and immediately knows 'I like these $130 lowers, pass on the $187 lowers,' that call comes from having seen those sections dozens of times, knowing what the market looks like, and recognizing the pattern without consciously running through every step. Building gut requires: (1) Watching drops actively even when not buying — see what gets called, what gets passed, and why. (2) Opening maps proactively on upcoming shows before anything drops. (3) Tracking what you bought and what it sold for. (4) Following tours across multiple shows, not evaluating each in isolation. Early on you don't have gut — you run the full checklist every time. That's correct and appropriate. As familiarity accumulates the checklist internalizes and decisions get faster. The goal is eventually making accurate decisions in the time it takes to glance at a price.

Insurance
An optional ticket protection add-on offered at Ticketmaster checkout — never select it.
More info

Ticketmaster and some other platforms present a ticket insurance option (and sometimes parking, event extras, etc.) during checkout. We never purchase these — skip past them every time. On fastcart accounts this prompt is bypassed automatically. On regular accounts, you'll need to actively decline it. It adds cost and is never relevant to our use case. Similarly, any other add-on (parking, fan clubs, charity donations at checkout) — decline all of them. The only thing you're buying is the ticket itself.

Inventory management
Managing what you've bought — tracking exposure, realistic upside, and when to cut losses.
More info

At any given time you have a portfolio of unsold inventory. Key things to track: (1) Total spend — actual dollars tied up in unsold tickets. (2) Maximum potential — if everything sold at current listing price, what's the total upside. (3) Realistic expectation — max potential is rarely achieved; realistic is typically 40–60% of max for a mixed portfolio. (4) Exposure by show — which shows represent the most risk if they crater. Quality matters more than quantity: $20K of well-chosen inventory is better than $50K where half is likely to eat. Inventory that hasn't moved by December is a concern — December is the best clearing window; what's left after December tends to crash. Proactively try to cancel seats you can no longer sell before the show date. Not all listings allow cancellation — if TM doesn't give you the option, you hold it. The goal is continuous buying of further-out inventory so your pipeline never runs dry.

JANCY
The browser we use for buying — holds all accounts, lets you launch listings across many accounts simultaneously.
More info

JANCY holds all your buyer accounts/profiles, each with a name, saved payment info, and ticket limit. Core workflow: drag or paste a listing URL into JANCY's URL bar → select which accounts to launch → it opens that page across all selected accounts simultaneously. For each open account tab: find the section, click the seats, add to cart. Status indicators: yellow = loading/carding in progress; green = confirmed in cart; blue/unchanged = didn't get in. Work through accounts one at a time — don't stop to confirm each one, just keep moving. When a batch is done, double-click to clear selected accounts and relaunch the same link to keep going. Two-window setup: some people run two JANCY windows — one for dragging in new links, one for launching accounts. Key shortcuts: Ctrl+Tab (move forward through tabs, click don't hold), Ctrl+Shift+Tab (backward), Ctrl+/ (toggle seat automation on/off — use if the auto-clicker stops responding), Ctrl+2 (autofill), Ctrl+F (search page for section number), Ctrl+T (new tab), Ctrl+W (close tab), F5 (refresh — use sparingly, refreshing too much triggers account blocks), right-click tab → close tabs to the right (clear a batch at once). Ticket limits are enforced by the underlying platform (Ticketmaster, etc.), not by JANCY — exceeding the limit gives the same error regardless. Launch small drops across multiple accounts anyway, not just one — some accounts simply won't see certain inventory, and launching wider increases your odds.

Juiced account
An account that gets an unusually early/low position number in a presale queue.
More info

When presale queues open, each account gets a random position. Most accounts get mid-range positions; occasionally one gets a very low number (e.g. position 50 vs. everyone else at 5,000+). That's a juiced account. The moment you identify one during a presale, tag it "juiced" immediately and set it aside. Do NOT use a juiced account for drops, and do not use it for other presales — save it exclusively for a top-tier presale where getting first access to the best seats matters most. Using it casually wastes its value entirely. Juiced accounts are rare and unpredictable — you can't engineer one, you just recognize it when it happens and protect it.

Launching on SeatGeek vs. Ticketmaster
SeatGeek currently has far fewer account blocks than Ticketmaster — easier access when a show is on that platform.
More info

Ticketmaster has aggressive rate-limiting and account-blocking infrastructure — refreshing too much, buying too frequently, or having a flagged account can get you blocked for minutes, hours, or permanently. SeatGeek currently has much less of this in place, meaning: (1) Shows on SeatGeek are easier to buy — you can launch more aggressively and face fewer blocks. (2) Completion rate per account is higher on SeatGeek than TM for equivalent effort. (3) When you see a show is ticketed on SeatGeek rather than TM, that's a slight operational advantage — less competition from blocked accounts and cleaner access. This may change as SeatGeek builds similar infrastructure, but as of now it's meaningfully more accessible. Always check the platform for each show before assuming TM buying strategy applies.

Lawn
Open grass seating at the back of an amphitheater — very large capacity but not technically unlimited.
More info

The back area of an outdoor amphitheater, general admission style (no assigned seats within the lawn). Capacity is large — a lawn that looks like a small strip on the Ticketmaster map can physically hold more people than the entire reserved seating area combined. Because of this large supply, lawns at full price are often not worth buying — there are simply too many seats available for secondary pricing to exceed primary by much. Exceptions: (1) Promo pricing — if lawns drop to a discounted price (e.g. $25 for seats normally $54), buy as many as you can and sell at or above normal price; the profit is from the price gap. (2) Hot show fully selling out — if a show is hot enough that all lawn tickets are gone or nearly gone, remaining/resold lawns can spike well above face value on secondary. Lawns are a quantity game, not a quality game — a small pack barely profits, but a few hundred lawn tickets at even $5–10 margin each adds up fast. The stock checker (purple alert in Phantom) is used for lawns specifically: admins set it to alert when stock drops to a level that suggests real demand (e.g. 1,000 remaining out of 10,000), confirming it's worth buying into.

Limited view
A seat with an obstructed sightline — a beam, pillar, speaker stack, staging, or other physical obstruction blocking part of the view.
More info

Ticketmaster doesn't reliably flag these on its own — you need to check the seat detail (scroll down on the section/seat listing) before buying. If a seat is limited view, you must disclose it on your resale listing. Failing to disclose means the buyer can dispute the purchase and receive a full refund while still attending the show — you lose the ticket's value twice over (refund + they keep the seat). Limited view seats typically sell for less than equivalent unobstructed seats in the same section. Related types with the same disclosure requirement: partially obstructed (some but not all seats in a section flagged), side view (angled view rather than straight obstruction), video view (no stage view at all — just a screen). The rule is: if you wouldn't be happy sitting there without knowing in advance, disclose it.

Make a Difference
A charity-linked ticket type where a portion of the purchase price goes to a designated charity.
More info

These appear on Ticketmaster and are priced higher than standard tickets specifically because of the charity component — the extra cost isn't buying you a better seat, it's a donation. Not a standard buy because: (1) the higher purchase price needs equally higher resale value to justify profit, and (2) buyers on the secondary market are typically comparison-shopping and won't pay a premium for the charity component. Market awareness and play catchup
Proactively learning shows, tours, and markets before drops happen — so you're never evaluating blind in real time.
More info

The best buyers aren't just fast — they've done the work before the drop. 'Play catchup' means deliberately studying upcoming shows so you're never seeing something for the first time when Phantom fires. Practical habits: (1) Look at everything on the drop checker within the next 2 weeks — those are the highest-likelihood drop candidates. Open their maps, check VividSeats, check DataIQ. (2) When a new tour is announced, immediately look at spec sales and ATP as they populate. Form an initial view before the presale opens. (3) Follow tours across multiple shows — don't evaluate City A's show in isolation, know how Cities B, C, D are performing on the same tour. (4) When you've seen a show drop before and watched what got called, you're building the pattern for next time. (5) Being aware of what's coming up in the next month means you're never surprised — you already know the artist, the venue, roughly what the market looks like, and what your plan would be if something dropped today. This is the single biggest leverage point for improving performance quickly.

Market flood
When a large sudden release of tickets crashes secondary pricing for a show.
More info

If 500 seats in a section drop at once, that section on the secondary market (VividSeats, StubHub) gets flooded with new supply. Sellers competing against each other drop their prices, and what looked like a $50 spread today might be a $26 spread tomorrow once everyone reprices. This is why the size of a drop matters — a small controlled drop (10–20 seats) is a much better sign than a massive release. Before buying into a large drop, think about what it does to the market: are there now so many seats available that the secondary price will crater? A good market check on DataIQ/TicketMetric right before buying can show if the ATP is holding or if it just dropped.

Mirroring section
The exact same section/row on the secondary market (VividSeats) as what you're considering buying on Ticketmaster.
More info

The starting point of every comp check: find the identical section and row on VividSeats (fees off, quantity 2) and compare the price. If VividSeats is running roughly 40%+ above Ticketmaster for that exact seat, there may be real margin. But don't stop at the mirroring section — also check every comparable and better section on VividSeats. If a better section (closer rows, higher tier) is listed at the same price as your mirroring section, no fan will buy yours over that one. Your seat is only a good buy if it still makes sense when compared against everything a buyer could choose instead. Think like the fan making the purchase decision, not the broker who already has the ticket.

MLB.com
Occasional primary marketplace for concerts held at baseball stadiums.
More info

We occasionally buy concert tickets through MLB.com when a show is hosted at a baseball stadium and routed through MLB's ticketing system (as opposed to Ticketmaster). This is rare and the platform is unreliable — checkout errors are common, and some shows simply can't be completed on it despite repeated attempts. Don't expect a smooth experience here. Still worth trying when it's the only source for a given show, but go in expecting friction and have patience. Not used for actual baseball games — only concerts at baseball venues.

New sections the drop checker can't catch
Floors and sections not yet on the map won't trigger drop checker alerts when they first appear — you need a price filter workaround.
More info

Phantom and all drop checkers can only monitor sections that already exist on the Ticketmaster map. If a venue section hasn't been added yet — like floor sections that haven't been released — the checker has nothing to watch. When those sections eventually drop for the first time, they won't trigger any standard drop checker alert because the system wasn't configured to look for something that didn't exist. Many buyers miss these entirely. The workaround: Set a manual price filter on the show with a max price below where you'd expect those sections to drop. Example: if you expect floors to drop around $200–300, set a max price of $250. If something drops below that threshold, it will hit your filter even though the section didn't exist in Phantom before — because the filter catches anything that falls inside the price range, regardless of whether it's a new section. If it drops for $251, it won't hit. This requires knowing ahead of time approximately what price point the new section might drop at. Example: Chris Brown / Usher floors hadn't been added to the Missouri map yet — anyone following the tour would know floors are coming, but without a price filter, no drop checker would catch the moment they first appeared.

No timer
A Ticketmaster checkout where the usual 7–8 minute countdown clock is absent — you must complete the purchase immediately.
More info

Standard Ticketmaster checkouts give you a countdown timer (usually 7–8 minutes) to complete the purchase before the seat is released back to inventory. Some shows — particularly Face Value Exchange listings — have no timer at all. The moment you click through to checkout, you need to complete it as fast as possible without any buffer to think. Strategy for no-timer checkouts: (1) use autofill (Ctrl+2) to fill the form instantly rather than typing, (2) use fastcart accounts when available since they skip the insurance prompt, (3) have already decided whether you're buying before you click through — the decision cannot happen during checkout. If you hesitate during a no-timer checkout, you may lose the seat. The core buying decision (is this market good? is this price right?) must be made before you enter checkout, not during.

Non-transferable

Official Platinum
Ticketmaster's dynamic-pricing tier — marked with an orange star on the map, priced well above standard.
More info

Official Platinum is Ticketmaster's own dynamic pricing program — the same seat as a standard ticket but priced higher based on demand algorithms. Often 2x or more the price of an adjacent standard seat. Key points: (1) Not the same as VIP — there are no extras like meet-and-greets, just a better/more desirable seat at a market-driven price. (2) Rarely a buy because the secondary market premium over a standard ticket rarely justifies the much higher Platinum cost. Exception: if the Official Platinum price is very close to the standard price (e.g. only ~$20 more), it can be worth taking over the standard seat since it's a better position for minimal extra cost. Always check the gap before automatically skipping past them. Marked with an orange star on the map.

Pit
The standing-room area directly in front of the stage at an amphitheater or arena show.
More info

Pit is the closest you can be to the stage — standing room, no assigned spots, general admission style within the section. Value varies enormously by artist and is NOT reliably predicted by genre alone, though trends exist: EDM/rave shows and rock/mosh-pit-culture shows tend toward strong pit demand (people want to be at the front regardless of physical comfort); some other genres are weaker. The specific artist matters far more than the genre. Pit tickets at high-demand artists have sold for $800+ when standard seats in the same venue went for $100–200. At a different artist at the same venue, pits might barely move. Before buying pit: check DataIQ/TicketMetric to see if pit tickets are actually transacting at a premium, and at what price. Pit is not always present — some shows have a seated floor rather than a standing pit, and some have both depending on the layout.

Playing safe vs. aggressive
The tradeoff between protecting yourself from big losses early on vs. maximizing upside once you have cushion.
More info

Early on — especially when buying drops rather than presales, without a large existing inventory to fall back on — playing conservatively is correct, not a weakness. If you go aggressive on something that crashes and you have no cushion, the loss hits hard with nothing to absorb it. As your cushion (profitable inventory and track record) builds, aggressive plays become viable: the downside of one bad call is manageable against the upside if you're right. At experienced levels, buyers regularly carry 5K–12K in monthly losses as a normal cost of doing business — offset by larger wins. The key variables that determine whether an aggressive play is right: (1) How strong is my existing cushion? (2) How confident am I in this specific play based on data, tour pattern, and market knowledge? (3) If this goes to zero, does it hurt me badly or can I absorb it? A bad month that takes out most of your cushion doesn't mean you played wrong — it means variance happened. The goal is that wins outpace losses over time, not that every bet wins.

Price break
A section split into multiple price tiers depending on row — closer rows cost more within the same section.
More info

A single section might have rows 1–10 priced at $300 and rows 11–20 priced at $180. The point where the price drops — between rows 10 and 11 in this example — is the "price break." When a team lead says "go for the price break," they mean specifically target the cheaper tier right at that boundary. Why: you're buying seats that are only marginally further back than the expensive rows, but at significantly lower cost — the resale spread is better. When choosing between tiers, think like a fan: would you pay $120 more to sit 5 rows closer? If yes (typical for primos and floors), take the better tier. If no (common for upper sections where the distance isn't meaningful), take the cheaper tier. Both tiers can sometimes be worth buying — closer rows at the top of the section can hold their own resale value even alongside the cheaper rows behind them.

Primo
The sideline sections in an arena's lower bowl — the best and most valuable seats in the building for most shows.
More info

Primos (also called sidelines) are the lower bowl sections that run along the sides of the arena, facing the stage from the side. They're considered the premium seats — closest to the stage action, best sightlines, and typically highest resale value. In a standard arena layout, the primos are the sections between the stage corners and the back of the lower bowl. Exact section numbers vary by venue — learn each arena's specific layout rather than assuming. When comparing sections, primo vs. corner vs. back bowl is the most common shorthand hierarchy. Primos almost always hold more resale value than corners or back bowl for the same row number.

Queue
The virtual waiting room you enter when a presale or on-sale opens — your position determines when you get in to buy.
More info

When a presale window opens, all accounts that launched the link join a queue. Each gets a position number (e.g. "You are number 8,432 in line"). The queue counts down as people ahead complete their purchases or time out. In JANCY, you can see the countdown across all your accounts simultaneously. Key facts: (1) Position is assigned randomly — you can't predict which account will get a good spot. (2) A juiced account is one that gets an unusually low/early position — rare, but when it happens, tag it and save it for top-tier presales. (3) Some accounts never get in even from decent positions if the show is extremely high-demand and inventory runs out. (4) Launching earlier (before the window opens) doesn't guarantee a better position — the queue randomizes when the window opens, not on launch time.

Reading a presale map
How to evaluate which sections to buy when you're in the presale queue and the map just opened.
More info

When you're in the queue and the map opens, you have to act fast. Two scenarios: If you've prepped (best case): You already know your target sections and price points from research done before the presale. You execute the plan — no deliberation needed. If the map is blank (nothing sold yet): This is the hardest case. You're committing without confirmation of where demand is. Use: (1) Spec sales from VividSeats/StubHub — where are people speccing and at what prices? (2) ATP from DataIQ/TicketMetric — what have people paid for similar shows? (3) Tour reference data — what sections performed well at prior shows? (4) Venue knowledge — where are the primos, where are the value sections? If it's a brand new tour with no reference data, rely on spec sales, ATP, and comparable artists at similar venues. Go for the sections with the best expected spread based on whatever data you have. High spec sales + high ATP = can be more aggressive. High spec sales + low ATP = demand for cheaps specifically, play accordingly.

Residency
When an artist performs multiple shows at the same venue over a period of time, rather than touring city to city.
More info

A residency means back-to-back shows in the same venue — the artist stays put while fans travel to them, rather than the other way around. Most commonly associated with Vegas: artists like Backstreet Boys, Adele, and Katy Perry have had Vegas residencies at venues like the Sphere or Caesars Palace, performing many consecutive shows. Billy Joel had a long-running monthly residency at MSG. The Sphere in Las Vegas is a common residency venue. A few things to know operationally: (1) The last shows of a residency sometimes have extra demand (closing-weekend effect) that inflates sales numbers — worth noting when reading DataIQ data for a residency run. (2) A residency rescheduled within a very short window (e.g. this Thursday moved to next Wednesday in Vegas) is particularly bad because Vegas is tourism-dependent — people built their entire trip around the original date, and those plans don't transfer to next week. (3) Residencies that are underperforming can make rescheduled shows even worse — if the overall run has been struggling, a reschedule within Vegas doesn't give the market time to recover.

Row 1 value
Front-row seats hold independent resale value even when the rest of that section is dead.
More info

Row 1 of any section — not just primos — has a distinct identity to buyers. Some fans specifically want front-row regardless of which section it's in: closest proximity to the barricade, unobstructed view, ability to interact with performers, get setlist papers, etc. This means Row 1 in a section where rows 2–20 are completely stagnant on secondary can still be selling at a meaningful premium. Worth checking specifically when a drop includes Row 1 even if the overall section looks marginal. Row 2 and Row 3 carry some of this premium but it diminishes quickly. In sections with letter designations (Row A, B, C), Row A is the equivalent of Row 1 — same principle applies.

SeatGeek

SeatGeek is also one of the secondary marketplaces TicketMetric pulls sales data from (alongside StubHub), which is why TicketMetric captures both broker and fan sales while DataIQ (which pulls from Automatic) captures only broker sales. Secondary market listings vs. actual sales
VividSeats listings are asking prices — not confirmed transactions. Only DataIQ/TicketMetric show what people actually paid.
More info

Every listing on VividSeats, StubHub, or SeatGeek is a price a seller is hoping to get — not a confirmed sale. A section with a cheapest listing of $250 does not mean fans are paying $250; it means someone is trying to sell at $250. The actual price people pay shows up in DataIQ and TicketMetric as ATP and individual sale records. This distinction is critical: a show can look great on VividSeats (high listings across all sections) while DataIQ shows zero sales in the past 30 days — meaning no one is actually buying at those prices. Always cross-reference listing prices with actual sales data before concluding a market is healthy. The combination you want: strong listing prices AND confirmed sales at those levels. Strong listings with no actual sales = speculative market that may not reflect real demand.

Show familiarity before a presale
Researching a show before the presale opens so you know exactly what to buy the moment you get in — not figuring it out in real time.
More info

Jumping into a presale map for a show you've never looked at is one of the hardest situations — nothing has sold yet, you don't know which sections move for this artist, and you're making real-money decisions in seconds. Preparation eliminates most of this. Before the presale: Check DataIQ and TicketMetric for spec sales and ATP. Look at the artist's other tour shows if they exist — sort by total quantity to see which cities are strongest. Look at comparable artists at similar venues. Identify your target sections and price points in advance. If it's a brand new tour: Rely on spec sales, ATP, Spotify monthly listeners relative to venue size, and city strength patterns you've observed. When you're in the map: You're executing a plan, not evaluating from scratch. If you haven't done prep, you're doing live evaluation during the presale with a timer running — a much harder and riskier position.

Singles strategy
When to buy single seats — generally avoided except for GA, Row 1, and genuinely hot shows.
More info

Singles (one seat alone with no adjacent companion seat) are hard to sell because most fans attend in groups. A pair fills one buyer's need completely; two singles require two separate buyers. However, singles are viable in specific situations: (1) GA sections — fans going solo can stand anywhere and pair up organically; a single GA ticket is functionally the same experience. (2) Very hot shows — when a show is genuinely in-demand (Ariana Grande, Noah Kahan peak demand), singles move because buyers are happy to get any ticket regardless of companion seating. (3) Row 1 singles — front-row seats sometimes sell individually because the Row 1 experience justifies the purchase. Outside these cases, default to pairs or larger packs. Going after singles aggressively early on (before knowing which shows support it) is a common new-buyer mistake.

Spec sales
Secondary market listings posted before the seller actually owns the ticket — people listing seats they expect to be able to buy, before the presale or on-sale opens.
More info

Spec sales (speculative listings) appear on VividSeats and StubHub before a show's tickets are even available to purchase. Sellers post listings at prices they think will be profitable, based on expected demand — they don't have the tickets yet. This creates a signal you can use when evaluating a presale or new tour announcement: strong spec sales with high prices indicate that sophisticated sellers expect the show to perform well. Weak spec sales (few listings, low prices) suggest less confidence in the market. Useful in combination with ATP from DataIQ/TicketMetric: strong spec sales + high ATP = can be more aggressive on that city; strong spec sales + low ATP = demand exists but for cheap seats, play accordingly; weak spec sales + low ATP = cautious or pass. Spec sales are one of the main tools for evaluating a brand new tour where there are no prior shows to reference. Important: spec sale listings are asking prices from people who don't have the tickets yet — they represent expected market, not actual transactions.

Spotify
Used to check an artist's monthly listeners as one signal of their popularity and market potential.
More info

Not a buying tool directly, but a quick and accessible proxy for an artist's audience size. 30 million monthly listeners playing an arena suggests a strong market; 2 million monthly listeners playing a stadium suggests a mismatch between venue size and actual draw — a risk signal for unsold inventory. Used as one input when evaluating whether to buy presale for an artist you don't know well, or when assessing a new show announcement. Not a standalone answer — an artist can have low Spotify numbers but a devoted live fanbase (e.g. jam bands, country), and vice versa. Combine with DataIQ/TicketMetric sales data for a fuller picture.

Standard Reseller
Ticketmaster's official resale program for listing tickets back on the TM platform itself.
More info

When you resell a ticket through Standard Reseller, it relists on Ticketmaster's own marketplace (buyers see it as a resale listing on the same Ticketmaster page where new tickets are sold). This is different from listing on VividSeats or StubHub — it stays within the Ticketmaster ecosystem. Standard Reseller listings follow TM's resale program pricing rules rather than pure open-market secondary pricing. Confirm specifics per event before listing since terms can vary. A Standard Reseller listing on the map appears with a pink/arrow marker, distinct from blue (original standard inventory) and orange star (Official Platinum).

Standard ticket
The regular primary inventory ticket — no extras, no special pricing tier, just the original seat at the original on-sale price.
More info

Shows as a blue dot on the Ticketmaster seat map. When you see "Standard" as the ticket type on a listing, it's original primary inventory — the artist/venue's regular tickets as they were released at the on-sale. Compared to Face Value Exchange (a fan returning their seat) and Verified Resale (already in TM's resale system), a standard listing at the same price is generally worth doing a VividSeats check before buying rather than auto-buying — it doesn't carry the same implied market signal. Standard tickets with a countdown timer give you the normal 7–8 minutes to checkout; some shows (and all Face Value Exchange) have no timer.

Stock checker

Sub-10 / sub-100
Instructions about row numbers — "sub-10" means rows under 10, "sub-100" means rows under 100.
More info

When a team lead says "go for sub-10" or "target sub-100," they almost always mean row numbers, not dollar prices. Sub-10 = rows 1 through 9 (very close rows); sub-100 = rows 1 through 99 (used in larger venues with many rows). If they mean a dollar amount, they'll typically state it explicitly with a dollar sign (e.g. "sub $200," "under $150"). Ticket limit
The maximum number of tickets one account can buy in a single order — enforced by the platform, not JANCY.
More info

Each account can only buy up to its ticket limit in one transaction — usually around 4–8 tickets, sometimes higher (up to 50 for some events). The limit is enforced by Ticketmaster/the underlying platform, not by JANCY — trying to exceed it gives the same "you've requested too many tickets" error whether you're buying through JANCY or directly on the site. To buy more tickets than one account's limit allows, you launch across multiple JANCY accounts simultaneously. Check the actual ticket limit on the map's right-side panel (the quantity selector) rather than assuming — it varies by event. For big drops where you want real volume, you need enough accounts to cover the math (e.g. 10 accounts × 8 ticket limit = 80 potential tickets).

Ticketmaster
The primary platform for ~85–90% of what we buy — the main primary marketplace for most major tours and arenas.
More info

Owned by Live Nation. The dominant platform for concert ticketing in the US. Key operational facts: (1) Delivery type must be set to Mobile — never Will Call. Will Call requires the buyer to physically pick up the ticket, making it unsellable as a resale. (2) Most checkouts have a 7–8 minute countdown timer; Face Value Exchange and some other listing types have no timer at all. (3) Ticketmaster doesn't always flag limited view, side view, or partially obstructed seats — check the seat detail before buying and always disclose. (4) Map dot colors: blue = standard inventory; pink/arrow = resale (Standard Reseller or verified resale); orange star = Official Platinum; gray = unavailable or not yet released. (5) Standard Reseller program allows listing tickets back on TM's own resale market. (6) Face Value Exchange is TM's fan-return mechanism — a fan who can't attend posts their seat back at exactly what they paid, no timer, non-transferable. (7) Never select insurance or add-ons at checkout. (8) Official Platinum listings are priced well above standard — rarely worth buying unless the gap to standard is small.

TicketMetric
Sales and pricing tool — captures both fan and broker sales, pulled from SeatGeek and StubHub.
More info

TicketMetric's data comes from SeatGeek and StubHub, which means it captures both professional broker sales and regular fan resales. This makes it broader than DataIQ (broker-only via Automatic). For niche, underground, or lower-profile shows where few brokers are active and DataIQ shows little or no data, TicketMetric often has more complete information because it's also capturing fan transactions. TicketMetric also shows individual recent sales by section and row in a feed format — useful for seeing exactly which parts of the venue are actively selling vs. sitting dead. Also has an artist-data feature showing monthly listener/follower counts by city — useful before a presale to identify which cities in a tour are strongest markets. Common workflow: check DataIQ first; if it looks borderline or shows thin data, check TicketMetric for a second perspective before passing.

Tour pattern recognition
Reading a full tour's performance trends to predict how new shows or upcoming drops will behave.
More info

Watching a tour unfold across cities builds a pattern that informs every subsequent buying decision. Key things to track across a tour: (1) Which cities outperform vs. underperform — some cities are reliably strong for certain artists (major markets, cities where the artist has a fanbase history), others consistently underperform. (2) Sales trajectory — is the tour gaining momentum as it progresses (later shows selling faster) or losing it (early shows strong, later shows softening)? (3) Flex patterns — if the tour has been flexing down lowers across multiple cities, expect that to continue in upcoming shows unless something changes. If it's been flexing up, that's a signal demand is outpacing expectations. (4) What sections move vs. sit — if primos are consistently the only sections selling and floors are sitting dead across multiple shows, that tells you where value is concentrated for that specific artist. (5) Week-of release behavior — is this tour consistently releasing floors the week of the show? Are they consistently pricing at $300 when they drop? If yes, you know exactly what to watch for and what to pay.

Uppers
The upper deck of an arena — cheapest seats, elevated view, rarely a priority buy.
More info

Uppers are the highest seats in an arena, furthest from the stage and at the top of the bowl. Generally the lowest resale value in the building for most shows. When to consider them: (1) If the show is extremely hot and all lower bowl and floor inventory is gone — uppers may be the only thing available. (2) If uppers drop at a very cheap price point where even a small secondary spread creates profit (e.g. uppers at $30 when secondary floor is $80). (3) For artists where uppers have historically moved because of the overall show strength. Generally: if you have a choice between uppers and lower bowl at similar costs, always take the lower bowl. Uppers can be a quantity play for genuinely hot shows. For most shows, they're the last thing you'd go for and often a pass.

Venue fit vs. artist popularity
Matching the artist's actual draw to the venue capacity — a mismatch is a major risk signal.
More info

One of the first things to assess on an unfamiliar show: does this artist's fanbase match the size of venue they're booked in? Use Spotify monthly listeners as a quick proxy. Underfill risk (danger): 2 million monthly listeners booked into a 20,000-seat stadium almost certainly can't fill it. Result: unsold primary inventory → venue/promoter drops prices → secondary craters → you're stuck. Avoid buying into shows where the artist can't plausibly fill the venue. Supply-demand imbalance (opportunity): 50 million monthly listeners playing a 2,000-seat theater has a massive mismatch in your favor — fans who can't get primary tickets will pay significant secondary premiums. These are ideal setups. The gray zone (most shows): The artist has a real fanbase, the venue is appropriate, and the question is whether this specific city/date will overperform or underperform — that's where DataIQ, TicketMetric, spec sales, and tour patterns give you the edge. Never evaluate venue fit in isolation; always combine with actual sales data.

Venue presale
A presale window tied to the venue itself rather than the artist or a credit card — runs through Ticketmaster on Thursdays.
More info

In the standard weekly presale pattern, venue presales run on Thursday at 10 AM local time for the show's city. These have their own unique code tied to the venue — not the artist, not a credit card. The code tends to be generic (anyone can use the same code) but is harder to find than an artist presale code. Main sources for finding venue presale codes: ShowsOnSale.com, AI tools, asking teammates. Because the code is generic once found, you don't need to have signed up ahead of time — you just need the code. Launch the link from the Google Sheet in JANCY and use the venue code when prompted.

Verified Resale
A resale listing within Ticketmaster's own marketplace — the original buyer is reselling through TM's system.
More info

Verified Resale listings appear on Ticketmaster with a pink/arrow marker on the seat map. These are tickets that have already been purchased and are now being relisted through TM's Standard Reseller program. Key differences from standard listings: (1) Generally considered a safer auto-buy candidate at reasonable prices — the seat is verified, already in the resale pipeline, and you know exactly what you're getting. (2) Face Value Exchange is a subset of this (fan returning at cost), but Verified Resale can also include above-face-value reseller listings. (3) No timer applies to Face Value Exchange specifically; Verified Resale listings may or may not have a timer depending on the listing type. Video view
A seat with no direct view of the stage — the buyer would watch the show on a video screen.
More info

The most restricted view type — worse than limited view or side view. Video view seats face away from the stage entirely and only have a screen to watch. We essentially never buy these. Important signal: venues only release video view seats for artists with extremely high demand — fans are buying just to be in the building even without a stage view. If you see video view seats on a map, treat it as a strong indicator that the show is very hot and all better inventory has already been consumed. Must be disclosed on any listing the same as limited view — undisclosed video view is a chargeback risk.

VIP package
A premium ticket bundle that includes extras like meet-and-greets, early access, or exclusive merchandise — priced well above standard.
More info

Different from Official Platinum (which is just a pricier seat with no extras): VIP packages actually bundle experiences or perks — backstage access, meet-and-greet, VIP lounge entry, exclusive merchandise, etc. The extras don't transfer meaningful resale value in most cases — a secondary market buyer is buying to attend the show, not to use a VIP lounge. This is why VIP packages are rarely worth buying: the purchase price includes the cost of those extras, but the resale price only reflects the seat itself. Exception case: if a VIP package's price is only marginally above a standard ticket and the seat position is genuinely better, it might be worth it — but this is uncommon. Always check VividSeats for the equivalent seat type before buying any VIP listing.

VividSeats
The secondary marketplace we use to compare pricing and determine if a buy makes sense.
More info

Default comp tool for all shows except Canadian and Broadway (which use StubHub). Always follow these steps when checking VividSeats: (1) Toggle fees OFF — prices default to showing with fees included, which inflates the number. With fees off, a $170 listing might actually be $140. Pricing off the fee-inclusive number is a common, costly mistake. (2) Default to checking quantity 2 — a single ticket is often priced misleadingly lower than pairs and doesn't represent the real market. Use quantity 4 if there's a lot of inventory for a more complete floor price view. (3) For Canadian shows, your Ticketmaster cost is in CAD — convert to USD before comparing since VividSeats shows USD. (4) Check the mirroring section first, then check comparable and better sections — your seat needs to hold up against everything a buyer could choose, not just the identical section. (5) StubHub is a useful second opinion if VividSeats pricing seems off or unusually high. Re-check markets you think you know regularly — they can change overnight, and a stale price in your head can lead to bad decisions on a drop.

Week-of releases by venue type
How likely each venue type is to release additional inventory in the week before the show.
More info

Stadiums: 100% guaranteed. Every stadium show without exception will release additional seats within the week of the show. This is the only category you can count on as a certainty. Plan around it — if a stadium show is coming up, there will be a drop. Know the market cold before it happens. Example: every single Chris Brown / Usher show has dropped at least 1,000 seats within the week of the show. Amphitheaters: Typically yes, but not guaranteed. They usually release something in the days before, but it doesn't always happen — especially for weaker shows. Arenas: Sometimes yes, sometimes no. Can't bank on it — depends heavily on the artist and how well the show is selling. Theaters: Least likely. Don't count on a week-of release for theater shows. This knowledge helps you prioritize: stadium shows in the next two weeks should always be on your radar because the drop is coming.

Weekly presale calendar
The standard Tue–Fri presale pattern that most major shows follow.
More info

Tuesday: Citi Card member presale — requires Citi card access.

Wednesday: Artist presale — run through Live Nation, the one you sign up for through the artist's official channels.

Thursday: Venue presale — tied to the venue, run through Ticketmaster, generic code (harder to find but anyone can use once found).

Friday: Public on-sale — no code, no presale access needed, anyone can buy.

Timing: All presales nominally start at 10 AM in the show's local time zone — not 10 AM Eastern. From Eastern time: East Coast shows (NY, Boston, Philly, Miami) = 10 AM ET. Central shows (Chicago, Nashville, Texas) = 11 AM ET. Mountain (Denver, Arizona, Utah) = 12 PM ET. Pacific (California, Nevada, Pacific Northwest) = 1 PM ET.

Important caveat: This 10 AM pattern holds roughly 90% of the time, but not always. Occasionally an entire show's presale schedule shifts later across the board (e.g. East Coast at 1 PM, Central at 2 PM, Mountain at 3 PM, Pacific at 4 PM). Don't assume 10 AM without checking the specific presale details.

Wheelchair-accessible
Designated accessible seating — almost never bought except for extremely hot shows.
More info

Wheelchair-accessible seats are designated for buyers with accessibility needs. We rarely buy these — the market for accessible seating on the secondary market is small and unpredictable. The exception is a genuinely exceptional show where all other inventory is gone and accessible seats are the only thing remaining — in that case, a hot enough market may move them. Must be disclosed on any resale listing as accessible seating. These typically have a companion seat alongside them (for an accompanying person) which affects how you'd list them.

Will Call
A ticket delivery method requiring the buyer to physically pick up the ticket at the venue box office — never select this.
More info

Will Call means there's no mobile transfer code — the ticket exists as a record in the original buyer's name, and that buyer (or someone with their ID) must go to the box office to pick it up. This makes the ticket completely unsellable as a resale in the traditional sense — you cannot send someone a mobile ticket if it's Will Call. Buying Will Call by mistake is effectively losing the entire purchase price. On Ticketmaster, delivery type is selected during checkout — always confirm it says Mobile before completing the purchase. If you're unsure, check the order review screen before clicking the final confirm button. This is one of the most costly and easily preventable mistakes, and it happens to almost everyone at least once.

World Cup
FIFA-run international soccer tournament — tickets purchased through FIFA.com/tournaments.
More info

World Cup tickets are bought through FIFA's own platform (not Ticketmaster or AXS). The process: find the match on FIFA.com/tournaments, click Buy Now, complete a captcha, enter the queue. Key differences from Ticketmaster: (1) FIFA has a site-level countdown timer showing how long you can browse before being kicked back to the queue — recently as short as ~9 minutes. Once you're in, you must move to checkout quickly. (2) Category system: Cat 1 = premium/lower bowl (yellow on map, highest price — sometimes several times more expensive than standard); Cat 2, 3, 4 = progressively worse seats at lower prices (Cat 4 ~$80). (3) Separate cart reservation timer once something is in your cart. (4) Move deliberately — clicking too fast may trigger blocks. (5) Geographic restrictions apply — certain cities and matches are off-limits; always verify the specific match before buying. (6) Autofill (Ctrl+2) may not fully work on FIFA — some fields need manual entry.
